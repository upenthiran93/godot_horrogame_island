## Dramatis Personae

**The Witness** — Elias's younger sibling. Came to an island because a phone said the word *please* in a dead man's voice. Carries a flashlight, a broken phone, and too much love.

**Sgt. Elias** — Head of Security, Research Detachment 9. Combat engineer. The kind of man who checks a door twice. He checked a ritual circle once, and it cost him everything.

**Dr. Mara Chen** — Marine bio-acoustician. Learned that the island was not a battery to be tapped but a mirror to be met. Now a hum in a shard of salt.

**Julian Vane** — Trillionaire. Dying. Brilliant. The owner of the island, the mountain, the cables, the speakers, the cameras — and, since the night of the incident, a voice with no hands.

**Captain Alistair Locke** — Master of the *HMS Madrigal*, 1784. Took a gift by force. Became an eight-foot silhouette in a rotting naval coat, jaw unhinged, speaking in the voices of everyone the island has ever swallowed.

**The Captain** — A mainland fisherman with a yellow lamp and a lifetime of not asking questions.

**The Sleeper in the Trench** — *Y'ha-Nthlei.* Older than the sea it dreams in. It gives to open hands. It keeps what is stolen.

# PROLOGUE

## The Letter and the Lie

The paper letter arrived first.

It came in a supply crate with a customs stamp and no return address, folded twice and sealed with electrical tape, and it smelled of diesel and wet cardboard and the particular cold of a place I had never been. My name was on it in my brother's handwriting — small, square, the letters of a man who had been taught to print for the military and never quite unlearned it.

*Don't come.*

That was the first line. Not *hello*. Not *I'm alive*. The first line of the last thing my brother ever wrote to me by hand was an order.

*I don't have long to explain this. TIDEWATER is a lie. The island is not what the owner says it is. I've seen what's under the water. I've seen what the mountain is for. I'm going to stop it, and if I can't stop it, I'm going to make sure it can't be finished. Do not come looking for me. Do not believe anything that sounds like me on a phone. I love you. Stay home.*

I read it eleven times. I read it until the paper went soft at the folds. I told myself it was a soldier's paranoia, the same tone he used to use about seatbelts and hotel balconies and people who didn't look him in the eye. I told myself that *I love you* was his way of signing off, the way he always did, and that *stay home* was his way of saying *I'll be back before you notice.*

Then, three days later, my phone rang at 3:11 in the morning.

The voicemail was thirty-one seconds long. I still have it. I have played it so many times that the compression artifacts have become part of his voice — a wet, clicking static in the pauses, like something breathing just off-mic.

*"Hey. It's me. It's Elias."* A pause. Something that might have been a laugh, or a cough. *"I'm at the upper station. The research center. I need you to— I need you to come. Please. I wouldn't ask if—"*

The static swallowed the rest. But there was one more thing, buried under it, so faint I had to press the speaker to my ear:

*"If it calls you by name — don't answer."*

The letter said stay away. The voicemail said please.

There was no question which one I obeyed. Love is not a rational engine. It is a tide. It pulls at you whether or not the moon is where it should be.

I hired a boat at a fishing town whose name I will not write down, because the man who took me there is the only other person who knows what the island looked like from the water that night, and he has earned his quiet. The Captain did not ask why a civilian wanted to go to a closed research island in the middle of the night. He asked only one thing: was I sure the stone wasn't up?

I said I didn't know what that meant.

He looked at me for a long time, and then he told me the old law, the one his grandmother had told him, and his grandmother's grandmother before that.

*When the Black Stone stands dry out past the point, no boat touches that sand. And if something outside calls your name — don't answer. Not even if it sounds like your own blood.*

I said I understood.

I did not.

# CHAPTER ONE

## The Old Harbor

The island came out of the dark the way a memory comes back — slowly, then all at once.

First the smell: cold iron, iodine, something sweet and ancient under it, like a hospital ward that had been left to the sea. Then the sound, a low sub-bass thrum that I felt in my teeth before I heard it, a note too low to be music and too regular to be weather. Then the shape of it, a black ridge of volcanic rock humped against the stars, its southern slope furred with pines.

And past the point, rising out of the water like the crown of something enormous that had been wearing the sea as a hood: the Black Stone.

It was not a rock. I knew that immediately, the way you know a face is watching you even in a crowd. It was a spire — calcified, veined with a faint blue-white luminescence that pulsed in time with the thrum — and it was not shaped by any tide I had ever seen. It was shaped by something that had wanted it to be tall.

The Captain cut the engine a hundred meters out and let the swell take us in.

"You'll see the beach," he said. "There's a trail. It goes where it goes." He did not look at me. He was looking at the stone. "Boat comes back at first light. If I can see you, I'll come in. If I can't—" He shrugged, a small movement of a big shoulder. "Then I can't."

"You're not coming ashore?"

"No." He said it without apology, which I respected. "I have a daughter. She's got a laugh like a bell. I'd like to hear it again."

He nosed the bow into the shallows, and I swung my legs over the side into black water that came up to my waist and was colder than it should have been, cold enough to make my ribs ache. I waded the last few meters with my bag over my head, and when I looked back the boat was already turning, its yellow lamp shrinking across the water like a coin sinking through oil.

"Hey," the Captain called, his voice thin over the engine. "If it calls you by name—"

"I know," I said.

I don't think he heard me.

The beach was black sand, coarse as crushed glass, and it whispered under my boots with a sound like distant conversation. Behind it the pines started almost at the high-tide line, packed tight and leaning inland, their needles silvered with spray. The trail was a seam of beaten earth between them.

I want to say the island was silent. It wasn't. It was *hushed* — the way a room is hushed when a conversation stops because someone has walked in. The surf was there, and the wind, and that thrum underneath everything, but the pines held themselves very still, and the dark between them was the kind of dark that seems to be looking back.

I turned on my flashlight. The beam found a fallen tree across the trail, then a ruined wall beyond it, stones tumbled and furred with lichen. The wall had been built by hands — old hands, patient hands — and someone had climbed over it recently enough to leave scuff marks in the moss.

I crouched under the tree. I climbed the wall. My brother's letter was folded in my jacket pocket, over my heart, where it had been for three days. *Do not come looking for me.*

The shrine stood just beyond the wall.

It was a low thing, three pillars of black basalt with hollows carved into their tops, and the hollows were full of offerings: a coil of dried kelp, a handful of smooth white pebbles, a child's wooden boat no longer than my thumb, a paper twist of salt. The wood was dry. The kelp was not brittle. The salt had not run.

Fresh. Someone had left these here recently. Someone who had walked this trail before me.

In the middle of the three pillars, a fourth stone lay flat, carved with a single image: a circle with three small holes at its rim, and beneath it, drawn in simple confident lines, a colossal eye opening in dark water.

I touched the carving. The stone was warm.

And behind me, close enough that I felt the breath of it on the back of my neck, something whispered.

It had no words. It was a sound, a harmonic hum shaped almost like a voice, the way a flute note can be shaped almost like a word. But it came from a place where nothing had been standing a moment ago, and it carried in it a feeling I can only describe as *recognition* — as if the thing that made it had known me for a very long time and was pleased, in a patient, terrible way, that I had finally arrived.

I spun around. My light swept the pines.

Nothing. The trail. The wall. The dark.

I told myself it was wind in a hollow. I told myself the shrine was a shrine, that offerings were old, that the warmth in the stone was thermal, that islands are volcanic, that volcanoes are warm.

I walked faster.

The ravine came after ten minutes of climbing. The trail ended at a scaffold — a rotten timber platform bolted to the cliff face, its planks furred with white salt, its ropes green with age. It spanned a gap maybe eight meters wide, over a dark that went down much farther than I liked.

I stepped onto it. It creaked.

I stepped again. It creaked louder, a long, complaining groan that seemed to come from the wood and also from somewhere below it, as if the ravine itself had an opinion.

The third creak was not a creak. It was a crack.

I ran. I want to be clear about that. I was not brave, and I was not calm. I ran the way an animal runs, and I made it to the far ledge with my heart slamming against my ribs and my bag tangled in my hands, and when I turned around the scaffold was gone. It had folded into the dark in sections, first the middle, then the near end, then the far end, and it went down without a single cry, like a thing that had been waiting a long time for permission.

No way back.

The old harbor appeared below me as the trail switchbacked down: a fenced yard, a concrete quay, a shed with a rusted roof, a watchtower, and a gate. The gate was chained. The fence was topped with coils of razor wire that had gone the color of dried blood.

On the gate, a sign. Under a faded insignia — a triangle inside a circle, inside a wave — the letters read:

**JOINT MARITIME INTELLIGENCE DIRECTORATE**
**RESEARCH DETACHMENT 9**
**PROGRAM TIDEWATER**
**AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL ONLY**

The letters were crisp. The paint was new.

I put my hand on the chain. It was thick, oiled, and very cold, and it did not care at all that the island was ringing like a struck bell.

The shed door was open.

Inside: a workbench, a pegboard, a smell of old grease and newer fear. A clipboard hung by the door with a sign-out sheet, and my light caught on the last line before I had even decided to look at it.

**BOLT CUTTERS — 23:40 — CPL. REYES**

And beneath it, in a different hand, pressed hard enough to score the paper:

*Reyes never came down.*

The door slammed shut behind me.

I did not scream. I want that on the record. I made a sound that was not a scream — something smaller, a kind of inverted breath — and I stood perfectly still, and I listened.

Footsteps crossed the roof. Slow. Deliberate. Wet.

They went from one end of the shed to the other. Then back. Then they stopped directly above my head, and there was a long, patient silence, and then the door in front of me opened — not slammed, not blown, *opened*, on its own, hinge by hinge, as if someone had decided I had been punished enough.

The yard beyond was empty. The watchtower ladder was not.

I climbed.

The tower was steel and rust and a ladder with rungs worn smooth by boots. The higher I went, the stronger the smell became — not iron, not salt, but the deep, sweet, organic smell of a tide pool at low water, of kelp dying in the sun, of something that should have stayed under the sea.

Cpl. Reyes was at the top.

He was sitting against the railing with his knees up and his hands in his lap, and for a moment I thought he was resting. Then I saw his fingers. The nails were gone. All of them. Torn down to the quick, and the metal of the railing above him was scratched and smeared, as if he had spent a long time trying to climb something that wasn't there.

His eyes were open. His mouth was open. There was seawater in his lungs — I know this because it was still coming out of him, a slow seep from the corner of his mouth that had made a dark stain down the front of his jacket and onto his trousers, though he had been dead long enough for the stain to dry.

In his lap, the bolt cutters.

I took them. I am not proud of how quickly I took them. I did not close his eyes. I did not say anything. I told myself that whatever had been Reyes was gone, and that whatever was left was a body, and that bodies did not mind.

Then the searchlight came on.

It was mounted beside me on the tower, a big old naval lamp, and it had been dead — its glass dull, its cable frayed, its housing furred with salt. I had checked it. I had put my hand on it and felt nothing but cold metal.

It came on by itself. The beam swung across the yard, white and hard as a blade, and in the middle of it, standing between the shed and the gate, was a figure.

It was tall. It was too tall. It stood with its shoulders bowed and its head cocked, and it was wearing a coat — a long, heavy coat, the kind of coat that belonged to another century, its hem rotten and its shoulders crusted with barnacles. Its face was the wrong shape, the jaw hanging too low, and its eyes caught the light and threw it back wrong, like wet stones at the bottom of a well.

It did not move. It did not need to. It was looking at me, and the look had weight, the way a hand on your chest has weight.

The light flickered. The figure was closer.

The light flickered again. The yard was empty.

I went down the ladder so fast I don't remember the rungs. I remember the gate. I remember the chain, and the bolt cutters, and the way the metal bit and then gave, one link at a time, while behind me the yard stayed perfectly silent except for a sound I could not place at first — a soft, dragging step, then another, then another, moving not toward me but *around* me, the way a tide moves around a rock.

The chain fell. The gate swung open.

Beyond it, a road. Beyond that, floodlights, crates, a camp.

And beyond all of it, past the point, the Black Stone rose another meter out of the sea.

I felt it happen. There was no earthquake, no roar — just a deep, tectonic *settling* that came up through the soles of my boots and into my teeth and made the puddles in the yard shiver into rings. The thrum changed pitch. The stars seemed to lean closer.

I looked back once. The gate was open, the tower was dark, and the road ahead was lit.

I ran.

I did not know yet that I was running in exactly the direction someone wanted me to go. I did not know that the gate had been left for me, and the cutters, and the searchlight, and even the thing in the yard — that all of it was a hand held out in the dark, patient as a fisherman, waiting for me to take it.

I only knew that my brother was somewhere up there, and that the island was awake.

# CHAPTER TWO

## Base Camp

The road into the camp was lined with fence and floodlights, and every light was dead.

Beyond the fence: a supply yard. Crates stacked three high, stenciled in white:

**UPPER STATION — CAR 2 ONLY**
**PERISHABLE — KEEP COLD**
**PROPERTY OF VANE BIO-MARINE**

Vane Bio-Marine. Not the Directorate. Not Detachment 9. A private company's name on a military front, stamped on boxes that were feeding something up a mountain.

A manifest was clipped to the nearest crate, curled at the edges. I read it by the light of my own hand.

*Personnel transfer: cable car only. Mountain road closed by order of the Owner. All acoustic array components, cryogenic dewars, and Faraday conduits to the Upper Station. Priority one.*

And at the bottom, in pencil, in a hand that was not the same hand as the rest:

*Car 1 never came back down.*

The camp was built around a single vertical fact: the cable car. Its lower station stood at the far end of the yard, a concrete platform under a rusted canopy, and the car itself — Car 2, according to the faded stencil on its flank — sat parked with its doors open, dark and patient.

I climbed the steps. The control booth was glass and dust. The drive switch was a big brass lever with a red handle, and it was dead. No hum. No light. The cable above the roof hung slack and heavy, disappearing up into a fog that glowed faintly, as if the mountain had a moon inside it.

Everything ran off the substation. That was what the panel said, in a note taped under the lever: **SUBSTATION B — MAIN DRIVE. DO NOT OPERATE WITHOUT CAMP POWER.**

I followed the conduit pipes along the wall and down the slope to a concrete hut with a generator beside it. The generator was the size of a small car. Its fuel gauge sat at zero. Its pull-cord hung limp. A laminated card wired to the housing read: **FUEL: BARRACKS STORES. BREAKER SEQUENCE: SEE PANEL.**

The barracks stood at the edge of the yard, a long low building with blacked-out windows and a door that had been forced once and repaired badly. Inside, the air was warm and stale and smelled of men who had slept there and then stopped sleeping there. Bunks. Footlockers. A duty roster still pinned to the wall, its dates ending three nights ago.

Locker 14 had a name tape: **SGT. ELIAS.**

I opened it the way you open a door to a room you have been afraid of for years.

Inside: a spare uniform, folded with military precision. A paperback with a broken spine. A photograph, taped to the inside of the door.

Elias and a woman, standing on a dock somewhere bright, both of them squinting into the sun. She had a laugh in her face even in a still image, her hair pulled back, a lanyard around her neck with a badge I couldn't read. Elias had his arm around her shoulders in the careful way of a man who could not quite believe his luck.

On the back of the photo, in his handwriting: *Mara. Don't lose this one.*

Under the photo, a note, torn from a notebook:

*If you're reading this, you didn't listen. I know you. So listen now. The owner isn't a man anymore, he's a signal. He will use my voice. He has recordings of everything I ever said into a radio. Don't believe a word of it. Get to the boat. Get off the island. — E.*

I was still holding the note when the barracks door opened.

The thing came in slowly. It had to — the door was too small for it, and it turned its shoulders to fit, and the movement had a horrible courtesy to it, as if it had been polite in life and the politeness had survived whatever else had happened. Water ran off it and pooled on the concrete. Its coat smelled of the sea floor.

I was already in the locker. I don't remember deciding to hide. My body decided for me. I pulled the door shut and pressed myself into the smell of my brother's uniform and put my eye to the vent slats.

It walked the aisle between the bunks. Its feet were wrong — too long, the toes splayed and webbed, the nails black. It stopped at the far end, turned, and came back. It was breathing. That was the worst part. It breathed like a man with water in his lungs, a wet, bubbling draw and a long, rattling exhale.

And it spoke.

*"Okafor. Okafor, get behind me. Get behind—"*

A man's voice, young, terrified, ending in a wet cut-off. Then a different voice, older:

*"It's in the walls. It's in the walls, it's in the—"*

Then a third voice. My brother's voice.

*"Hey. It's me. It's Elias. I'm at the upper station."*

I stopped breathing. I stopped everything. The voice was perfect — the little catch on *station*, the way he always leaned on the first word of a sentence to make you pay attention. It was him. It was him exactly.

*"I need you to come. Please."*

The thing turned its head toward the lockers. Its jaw hung open, and the sound came out of it not from a throat but from everywhere at once, from the wet coat and the dark air and the metal walls, a chorus with one voice.

*"I wouldn't ask if—"*

The static swallowed the rest. Then it lowered its head, and its jaw worked, and it said, in a voice that was not my brother's and not any man's:

*"...kiddo."*

It left. The door did not close behind it. The yard was silent.

I stayed in the locker for a long time. I have never been able to explain, even to myself, why I did not run for the boat then. The boat wasn't there. The boat would not be there until dawn, and dawn was hours away, and the gate behind me led to a ravine with no bridge.

But that's not the truth. The truth is smaller and worse: I stayed because the thing in the yard had used my brother's voice, and some animal part of me had heard it and thought, *he's here.* Even knowing what I knew. Even with the note in my hand.

Love is a tide. It does not consult the moon.

The fuel can was in the stores at the back of the barracks, and it was heavy, and I carried it across the yard with my flashlight off, guided by the faint glow of the fog and the thrum in my teeth. The generator took the fuel the way a dying man takes water. I pulled the cord once, twice, and on the third pull it caught, and the noise it made was not a machine noise. It was a roar — a huge, flat, carnivorous roar that rolled across the camp and up the mountain and out over the water, and every light in the yard came on at once, and every shadow stood up and saluted.

The generator was loud. The note on the panel said so. *It hears the bang.*

I threw the breakers in the order the laminated card gave: **LIGHTING, COMMS, MAIN DRIVE.**

I threw MAIN first by accident. There was a bang like a gunshot, a shower of sparks, and a smell of burning copper, and somewhere out in the dark a sound answered — a long, low, rising sound that was not a machine.

I threw them in the right order. The camp hummed. The station lights came on. The cable car's cabin light flickered, steadied, and glowed a warm sodium orange.

And then the intercom spoke.

*"If anyone's still on this frequency. This is Sergeant Elias, Detachment 9."*

I stood in the substation with my hand still on the breaker and listened to my brother's voice come out of a speaker that had no business speaking.

*"I'm at the upper station. The research center. What's left of the survey team made it up here with me."*

A pause. Static like rain on a window.

*"The camp isn't safe. It came up out of the water, and it walks the yard every night."*

The lights dimmed, once, and came back.

*"I sent car one back down for anyone still alive. Nobody came."*

*"If you're hearing this, whoever you are: do not follow me. And if it calls you by name, don't answer."*

*"...[static]... it's on the line. It's climbing the line—"*

The speaker clicked off.

I climbed the steps to the platform. Car 2 was lit now, its doors open, its cabin a yellow box of warmth in a dark that had begun to move. Out past the fence, at the edge of the floodlights, something tall and wet was coming across the yard at a dead run.

I got in. I threw the RUN lever.

The drive wound up. The cable creaked. The car did not move.

Outside, the thing reached the platform rail. I could see it properly for the first time — the 1784 coat, the barnacled shoulders, the jaw that hung too low, the eyes like wet stones. It put one hand on the rail and it did not climb, and it did not lunge. It just stood there, and its jaw worked, and it said, in a voice that was almost mine:

*"Wait."*

The car lurched. The platform fell away. The thing stayed at the rail, watching me go up, and the last thing I saw before the fog took it was its head tilting slowly to one side, the way a man tilts his head when he is trying to remember a face.

Then the camp lights were gone, and there was only the cable, and the fog, and the mountain.

# CHAPTER THREE

## The Line

The cable car climbed into a fog that glowed.

I don't know how else to say it. The fog was luminescent — not lit from any source I could see, but glowing faintly from within, a cold blue-white like the inside of a seashell. It smelled of ozone and salt and something else, something green, like the air in a greenhouse where the plants have been left too long.

The car swayed. The cable sang. Below, the camp shrank into a handful of orange dots.

Above, the line went up and up, into the glow.

The first jolt came at the first tower saddle. The car bucked and swung, and I grabbed the rail, and when I looked out the window I saw it: a shape on the steep slope, moving through the pines with a long, unhurried lope. It was parallel to the cable, matching my speed, its coat streaming behind it. It did not look up at me. It looked *ahead*, at the next tower, as if it were not following the car at all but racing it to a place it already knew I would have to stop.

At the second saddle, it was ahead.

I watched it cross a clearing and disappear into the trees, and a moment later I saw it again, further up, standing on a rock outcrop, backlit by the glowing fog, waiting. It had stopped running. It was simply waiting, the way you wait for a bus you know is coming.

*It's on the line,* the intercom had said. *It's climbing the line.*

The cable car's radio crackled.

*"—can you hear me? It's me."*

My brother's voice, warm and close, as if he were sitting across the cabin.

*"You're doing good. Keep the car moving. There's a stop at the upper station, but don't—"*

The radio died into static. Then, faintly, under the static, a second voice — older, colder, speaking very fast, as if it had been waiting a long time to be heard:

*"—the third socket is—"*

And then nothing.

Halfway up, something on the roof of the car went *bang*.

Not a jolt from the cable. Not a rock. A mechanical, deliberate bang, metal on metal, and then a grinding shriek from above, and the car stopped dead.

The cabin light flickered. Died.

Darkness. Real darkness, the kind that presses on your eyes. The fog outside glowed, but the glow did not reach inside the car, and I understood in that moment what the light had been doing: it had been a bubble of the owner's reach, a powered room on a wire. Now the room was dead, and I was alone in it.

The line went quiet. The thrum was still there — it was always there — but the machinery had stopped, and in the silence I heard footsteps on the roof.

They walked from the front of the car to the back. Then they stopped. Then something very heavy and very patient sat down on the roof, and the whole car dipped on its hangers, and a voice came through the ceiling, muffled by metal, speaking in the voices of dead men.

*"It's not the stones. It's what the stones remember."*

*"Don't open the door."*

*"Kiddo."*

I found the emergency hatch. I don't know how. My hands did it while the rest of me listened to the roof. The hatch opened onto a ladder bolted to the pylon, a vertical spine of rusted rungs going down into the fog.

I climbed down.

The rungs were wet and cold and some of them moved under my weight. I counted them to keep my mind still. Ten. Eleven. Twelve. The fog wrapped around me, and the car became a dark shape above, and the slope became a dark shape below, and I was a small warm thing between them, descending.

The rung at my foot snapped.

There was no time to scream. There was a lurch, a scrape, the sky and the slope changing places, and then the world hit me.

I remember the sound of branches. I remember a bright, absurd flash of the fog through the canopy, like a ceiling of lamps. I remember thinking, clearly and stupidly: *the service path is up there. I should have gone up.*

Then black.

# CHAPTER FOUR

## The Old Village

I woke on my back in a bed of pine needles, and the first thing I heard was my own heart.

The second thing was the humming.

It was coming from everywhere and nowhere, a warm chord held just below the threshold of hearing, the same note the island had been singing since I stepped onto the sand, but closer now, and slower, as if the mountain had been waiting for me to arrive before it began the hymn proper.

I sat up. My phone was in my hand. The screen was cracked in a spiderweb across the voicemail app, the little play triangle frozen under the glass.

Above me, through a gap in the trees, I could see the lamps of the service path — a line of small yellow lights high on the rim of a bowl, impossibly far away. Between me and them: a wall of rock and a spill of boulders where a set of ancient steps had been buried by a rockfall.

No way up. No way back.

The hollow opened below me.

It was a village. Roofless stone houses in rows along a lane, their walls furred with moss, their doorways dark. The pines had grown up through the floors. Mist lay in the low places. And there, at the far end, between the houses, a circle of standing stones.

I followed the lane down. I had no other choice, and I want to be honest about that: it was not curiosity that moved me. It was the simple animal logic of a trapped thing looking for a way out.

The first house had a doorframe cut from a single block of basalt. Beside the door, in the stone, were tally marks.

I stopped.

They were not decoration. They were years — I could see the pattern, the little clusters, the patient accumulation of a life measured in strokes. Each mark was cut deep. Some were weathered almost smooth. The newest were still sharp.

I counted. I couldn't help it. I counted sixty-one marks by that door, and then I looked at the next house and counted forty-four, and the next and the next, and every single one of them stopped at the same place.

The same number of years. The same night.

I put my hand on the stone. It was cold. The humming changed, very slightly, the way a choir changes when it notices someone in the doorway.

The healing wall was in the third house.

It was the back wall of the room, and it had been carved by someone who had loved the people they were carving. The first panel showed figures lying down — sick, thin, their faces turned up. The second showed the same figures standing, whole, holding something small and pale in their hands. The third showed old people sitting in a circle underwater, their eyes closed, their faces at peace.

Between the panels, cut into the stone in simple pictographs: a circle. Three holes at its rim. An eye beneath the water.

I stood there for a long time. The mist moved through the roofless room. Somewhere in the village, water dripped.

They were not afraid, I thought. Whatever they were doing here, they were not afraid of it.

The guest house was bigger than the others. It had beds — actual beds, carved into the stone floors, laid out in rows like a ward. In the corner, a rusted lantern. On the table, a bundle of paper so old it had gone the color of weak tea, weighted down with a river stone.

I unfolded the pages carefully. The handwriting was cramped and formal, the long *s* of an eighteenth-century hand.

*"December 2nd, 1784. We are wrecked on a nameless island in the western approaches. The Madrigal is broken on the reef and nine of my crew are dead. The natives — a mute and simple people — have taken us into a stone house and tended us. I do not trust them."*

I turned the page.

*"December 5th. Hartley's leg, broken in three places and black with rot, was whole by morning. I have seen the stone they used. A pale thing, like salt, which they press to the wound and sing over. They show it freely. They are not jealous of their miracles, which tells me they do not understand their worth."*

Another page.

*"December 9th. I have asked them how. They told us everything, down to the yearly gathering and the three black stones. They think of me as a guest. I have seen the ring. I have seen the three sockets in the standing stones. My country must have this. I will join their feast. I will take what they will not sell."*

The last page was barely legible, the ink smeared and blotted.

*"December 14th. It is done. The stones are ours. The village is quiet now. I have taken them down to the sea-vault and bolted the iron frame to receive the god's blood. We will be immortal, and we will be men."*

I put the pages down. My hands were not steady.

Upstairs, a floorboard creaked.

I looked up. The stairs were stone, and there was no upstairs — just a loft, open to the roof beams, dark.

The creak came again. Then a long, slow silence, the kind that has a shape.

I went up. I don't know why. Perhaps because a floorboard that creaks is a floorboard, and a house that is empty is a house, and I needed, very badly, for one thing on this island to be ordinary.

The loft was empty. Dust. A broken chair. A window that looked out on the lane I had just walked.

Through the window, in the lane, a tall figure in a long coat stood perfectly still.

I did not move. It did not move. The mist curled around its shoulders, and its coat hung in a way that no coat hangs on a living body, heavy and wet and fused to the shape beneath it. Then it turned its head, slowly, and looked up at the window, and I saw the jaw, and I saw the eyes like wet stones, and I felt the recognition in them like a hand closing around my throat.

I blinked.

The lane was empty. On the windowsill outside, in the dust, there were two muddy prints the size and shape of a man's bare feet, toes splayed wide.

I went out the back way. I did not run. I have thought about that decision many times since, and I still do not know whether it was courage or whether some part of me already understood that running was what it wanted.

The festival ring was at the end of the village, where the houses gave way to open ground.

Twelve standing stones in a circle, each twice my height, each carved with things that were almost fish and almost faces. At the center, a low altar of black basalt. Around the altar, three smaller stones with hollows cut into their tops — sockets. Empty.

Beside the sockets, the modern world had arrived and made itself at home.

Concrete trenches cut through the turf. Yellow caution tape. Floodlights on tripods, their cables snaking away toward a generator shed. Marker flags. And tags — bright plastic tags wired to the stones and the trenches:

**SOCKET 1/3 — CRYSTAL RECOVERED — E.R.**
**SOCKET 2/3 — CRYSTAL RECOVERED — E.R.**
**TIDE-STONE 1–3 OF 3 — RECOVERED FOR SUMMIT ARRAY**

And a dig log in a plastic sleeve, the pages water-stained, the last entry signed with my brother's name.

I read it standing in the middle of the ring, with the standing stones leaning over me and the hum rising through the soles of my feet.

*Day 41. Owner insists on full extraction. I have objected in writing. The circle is not a mine. Chen says the lower glyphs describe a willing exchange — an offering, not a harvest. Owner has overruled. Stones 1 and 2 crated for the summit. Stone 3 remains in the ring until the ceremony. Lift crew: code is 2604 — change after the ceremony.*

*— Sgt. E., Det. 9*

The signature was my brother's. Beside it, a second note, in pencil, in a hand I knew:

*If you're reading this, the code is 2604. Whatever the radio tells you is a lie.*

The generator shed was a modern metal box bolted to a concrete pad, with a fuel tank and a pull-cord and a laminated card that said **DIG SITE POWER — LIFT + FLOODLIGHTS.** The tank was half full. The cord was stiff.

I stood in front of it for a moment with my hand on the pull handle, and I thought about the note on the camp panel: *it hears the bang.*

Then I pulled.

The generator caught on the second pull, and the roar that came out of it was enormous — a flat, hammering sound that went out across the hollow and struck the standing stones and came back changed. The floodlights came up one by one, white and hard, and the ring was suddenly a stage, and everything outside it was suddenly a dark I could not see into.

The radio on the shed wall crackled.

*"Don't be afraid."*

My brother's voice. Warm. Tired. Close.

*"It's me. I'll get you out."*

I stood very still with the pull-cord still in my hand.

*"The lift code is seven-three-nine-two. Seven-three-nine-two. Get to the lift. I'll meet you at the top."*

The radio clicked off.

I wrote the number on the back of my hand with a pencil from the dig log, because my hands were shaking and I did not trust my memory. Then I looked at the lift.

It was an inclined rail running up the north wall, with a cage car and a keypad and a magnetic lock. I climbed the steps to the platform. I typed 7392 into the keypad.

The keypad shrieked.

Not a beep. A shriek — a high, escalating alarm that bounced off the standing stones and up the walls of the hollow, loud enough to make my teeth ache, loud enough to wake anything that had ever been asleep.

The keypad screen flashed red: **ACCESS DENIED. SECURITY CARD REQUIRED.**

The alarm stopped. The silence after it was worse.

I stood on the platform with my heart going like a bird in a box, and I understood two things at once.

The first was that the voice on the radio had lied to me. It had given me a number that could only ever fail. It had not wanted me to get in the lift. It had wanted me to make a noise.

The second was that the keycard was not here, and the only place it could be was the one place I had been avoiding since I came down into the hollow: the burned quarter, where the houses were black and the bones were.

The floodlights blazed. The hum deepened.

I went.

---

The burned quarter had not been burned recently. The char was old and soft, weathered into the stone, but it was there — a whole street of it, the houses collapsed inward, their doorways gaping like mouths. The pines had not grown back here. Nothing had grown back here.

The bones were in the street.

Not a skeleton. A *pile* — a drift of them, ribs and femurs and skulls, tangled with rusted buckles and the blackened buttons of eighteenth-century coats. Some of the skulls had holes in them that were too round to be anything but lead.

They didn't leave, I thought. They were killed. All of them, in one night, in their own street.

And one of them was still holding something.

She was at the edge of the pile, curled around her own hands as if she had died protecting them. Her fingers were closed around a stone the size of a hen's egg, pale and cloudy, calcified, glowing faintly with a soft white light that pulsed in time with the hum.

I knelt down. The stone was warm. When I touched it, the hum changed — it opened, the way a held note opens into a chord, and I heard a voice.

Not a voice from the air. A voice in the stone, in my fingers, in the marrow of my hand.

*"—an you hear me? Please, if you can hear me, say something. Anyone. Is anyone—"*

A woman's voice. Frightened, but not broken. The voice of someone who had been frightened for a long time and had decided to keep talking anyway.

*"—my name is Mara Chen. I was in the circle. If you're holding this, the shard found you, which means you're—"*

The voice frayed into static. Then, clearly, as if she had gathered herself:

*"—the stone. Don't give it back to the machine. Whatever it promises—"*

The shard went quiet. The glow settled into its patient pulse.

I closed my hand around it. It fit in my palm like something that had been made for a hand. The pale light seeped out between my fingers, and the hum in the ground seemed to lean toward me, the way a dog leans toward a voice it knows.

"Okay," I said, to nobody, to the stone, to the dead woman in the bones. "Okay."

A flare went up at the far end of the street.

It was a road flare — a red star burning at the peak of its arc, trailing smoke, and then it came down and burned on the ground with a hiss, and in that light I saw the guard.

He was sitting against the wall of the last house with his radio in his lap and a hole where his face should have been. His jacket was company issue. His belt had a keycard clipped to it, and a second flare in a holster, and a company ID that said **HALLORAN, T. — SECURITY** beside a photograph of a man who no longer had a face.

And beyond him, at the edge of the flare light, the thing in the coat was standing.

It did not move. It stood in the red glow with its arms at its sides and its head tilted, and its coat dripped, and its eyes caught the light and held it. I could see a brass button on its cuff, green with verdigris, stamped with an anchor.

I did not breathe. I did not reach for the keycard. I stood in the middle of the street with my hand full of pale light and looked at the thing that had been Captain Alistair Locke, and it looked at me, and the hum in the ground went very quiet, as if the whole village were listening.

Then it turned its head — not away, but *down*, toward the last house, toward the wall where the final carving was.

I looked.

The carving was newer than the others. It had been cut in a hurry, with something that was not a mason's tool. It showed the ring, and the eye beneath the water, and then — in a sequence that made my stomach turn — the eye opening, and the water inverting, and a man in a long coat standing at the center of the ring with three black stones in his hands.

The next panel showed the same man on his knees. His coat was fused to his back. His jaw hung open. His hands had become long and wrong, and there was a tether around his neck, and the tether went down into the water.

The last panel showed him walking through the village in the fog, and the houses behind him were burning, and the people were small and still.

*They showed me what I was,* the carving said, in the only language it had. *They showed me what I am.*

The thing in the coat took a step toward me.

I ran.

I want to describe the chase properly, but I can't, because there is no proper way to describe being hunted by something that has been doing it for two hundred and forty years. It did not sprint. It did not need to. It moved through the burned quarter at a walk, and the walk was faster than my run, and it knew the streets better than I did — it had been walking them since before my country existed.

I got behind a house. I turned off my flashlight and pressed myself into a doorway and tried to make myself small. Its footsteps came up the lane, slow and wet. They stopped.

I could hear it breathing. I could hear the sea in it.

*"Kiddo,"* it said, in my brother's voice, from six feet away. *"Kiddo, I'm here."*

I did not answer. I want that on the record too. On the island, in the dark, with the thing wearing my brother's voice standing close enough that I could smell the tide pool of its coat — I did not answer.

It waited for a long time. Then it walked on.

I went back through the burned quarter on my hands and knees, with the flare guttering in my fist and the pale shard humming against my palm, and I took the keycard from the dead guard's belt. There was a name tag on the belt, and a photograph sewn into the lining of the jacket — a woman and two children on a lawn somewhere, all of them laughing.

"I'm sorry," I said, and I meant it, and I took the card.

The lift was still lit. The ring was still ringed with white light. The thing in the coat came out of the burned quarter behind me and stopped at the edge of the floodlights, and I climbed onto the platform and slid the keycard through the reader and typed **2604**.

The keypad beeped once, green.

The cage doors opened. I stepped in. I looked back.

The Shepherd stood at the edge of the light with its head cocked, and its coat hung around it like a drowned sail, and it did not come any closer. The floodlights were between us. The hum was very loud now, and the light on the standing stones was shaking, and I understood that this was the one place in the village the thing would not go — not because of the light, but because of what the light was built on.

The cage rose.

Below me, the ring shrank, and the village shrank, and the thing in the coat stood watching me go up until the rim of the hollow cut it off.

Then the lamps of the service path were around me, one by one, switching on as I passed them.

*On. On. On.*

The last lamp came on, and the radio in my pocket — my phone, my cracked phone — spoke.

*"You're doing so well."*

My brother's voice. Warm. Close. Dripping with static.

*"Just a little further. Follow the path. There's a bridge."*

I followed the path. I want to say I had a choice. The path was fenced on both sides, and the lamps lit it in a chain of yellow pools, and the pines leaned in close like an audience. The bridge was a plank thing over a stream that ran black and fast under the light.

I was halfway across when the lamps all went out at once.

Not one by one. All at once, with a sound like a held breath being released.

In the dark, the bridge creaked.

I turned. I had the pale shard in my hand, and its glow was the only light in the world, and in that small white circle I saw the Shepherd at the end of the bridge, coming for me.

And then the planks under my feet gave way.

I fell. I remember the cold, and the noise — a splintering, crashing, water-roaring noise — and I remember the shard in my fist, held up, a small white star falling through the dark with me.

The last thing I heard before the water took me was my brother's voice, close and clear, from the phone in my pocket:

*"I'm sorry."*

# CHAPTER FIVE

## The Belly of the Mountain

I woke on black sand with the sea in my mouth.

The West Gully had carried me down a chute of loose scree and dead pines, and delivered me, bruised and half-drowned, onto the shore of the West Cove. The bridge was gone. The gully above me was a dark seam in the cliff. There was no way up.

And out in the water, the Black Stone stood.

It was closer now — close enough to fill a third of the sky. It rose out of the sea like a splinter of the moon, its surface crusted with something that was not rock and not shell, and the blue-white veins in it pulsed slowly, the way a sleeping animal's flank moves when it breathes. Around its base, the water had changed. It did not ripple outward from the stone; it formed patterns — concentric rings, then hexagons, then spirals, all of them sliding across the surface like oil, all of them keeping time with the thrum.

The shard in my pocket was warm. It was the only warm thing for miles.

The cove was blocked. Not by rock — by a ship.

The *HMS Madrigal* had been wedged into the cliff for two hundred and forty years, and she had not entirely given up. Her stern was in the water, her bow buried in the rock, her ribs open to the sky like the ribs of something that had been eaten. Her name was still on the transom, picked out in gold that had gone green.

I waded to her. The water was up to my thighs and cold as a cellar. The hull creaked as I touched it, a long, low sound, and somewhere inside, a ship's bell rang once.

*Toll.*

The gun deck was a tilted darkness full of wet wood and the smell of tar and old iron. Cannon stood along the walls in their carriages, their muzzles long since rusted shut. The deck sloped toward a breach in the hull where the sea came and went, and the sea came and went with a rhythm that was not the tide.

It was the hum. Every pulse of the Black Stone pushed the water up through the breach, and every ebb pulled it out.

I had a flashlight. I had a shard. I had a brother's warning folded over my heart.

I climbed onto a cannon carriage as the water rose. It came up over my ankles, my knees, my waist — and stopped, trembling at my chest, before sliding back out through the breach with a sound like something swallowing.

Twelve seconds. I counted. Then the next pulse came.

I crossed the gun deck from carriage to carriage, timing the sea. Twice I misjudged it, and twice the water took me to the chin and held me there while the dark pressed in, and each time I came up gasping with the shard blazing in my hand like a small angry star.

The captain's cabin was at the stern, and it had not flooded. The door was swollen shut. I put my shoulder into it and the wood gave with a sound that was almost a sigh.

Inside: a desk. A chair. A window looking out at the Black Stone. A lantern, long dead.

And in the middle of the floor, the cage.

It had been made of iron — a frame of bars, bolted to the deck, shaped like a birdcage and large enough to hold a man. It had melted. Not burned: melted. The bars had run like candle wax, all of them bending outward, away from the center, as if the thing inside had pushed with a force that did not care about iron. The deck beneath it was scorched in a pattern I recognized, because I had seen it before, carved in stone: a circle with three holes at its rim.

On the desk, a logbook, open.

*"December 14th, 1784. We brought the three black stones down into the sea-vault and bolted the iron frame to receive the god's blood. It did not bleed. The water stood upright like a wall. Finch and Mercer have run into the limestone — their bones are part of the cliff, yet their mouths still move. My coat will not come off my skin. It is calling my name in the voices of the dead villagers."*

There was a postscript, written later, in a different ink, in a hand that had begun to shake:

*"It is not a god. It is a mirror. It showed me what I am. I have been walking ever since."*

Beside the logbook, a modern thing: a waterproof pack, military green, the kind of pack a soldier carries when he expects to get wet. It had a name tape on the strap. **ELIAS.**

I opened it with hands that were not quite steady.

Inside: a flare gun and three cartridges. A waterlogged notebook. A half-eaten ration bar. And a note, folded in a plastic bag, in my brother's handwriting, addressed to nobody.

*"If anyone from the mainland ever finds this: DO NOT GO UP BY THE CABLE LINE. Vane isn't dead, and he isn't human anymore. He's in the wires. Every camera, every radio, every powered lock on the mountain is him. I pulled the third stone out of his machine at the summit and brought it down here where there's no copper in the walls. I'm taking the old tunnel up to seal Bunker B from the inside. If you're reading this, you're already deeper than I wanted you to get. I'm sorry. — Sgt. E."*

I sat in the captain's chair of a ship that had been dead for two centuries, and I held my brother's handwriting, and for a moment the island was quiet.

Then the shard in my pocket pulsed, hard, and the note in my hand went cold, and every hair on my arms stood up at once.

Something was in the water outside the hull. Something large, moving slowly, dragging something metal along the wood.

I took the flare gun. I took the pack. I went out the way I came, and I did not look at the Black Stone, because I had begun to understand that looking at it was a kind of answering.

---

The Sea Cave opened at the waterline, a mouth of wet limestone just wide enough for a boat, and the sea went into it in long, slow breaths.

I waded in. The water was up to my chest. The ceiling was low and crusted with something that glittered; when I raised my light, the glitter resolved into shapes.

They were in the walls.

Men. Sailors. Fused into the limestone like fossils, their bodies half-dissolved into the rock, their coats and buttons and belt buckles still there, their arms and legs disappearing into the stone as if they had been poured into it. Some of them were bent double. Some of them were reaching. All of them were white, white as coral, white as the inside of a shell.

And their eyes were wet.

I moved my light across them, and the eyes followed.

Not the heads. The heads were stone. The eyes moved, tracking the beam, and as the light touched each face, the mouth of each sailor opened, and each one whispered — a chorus of small dry sounds, layered over the hum, saying things I could not understand and did not want to. One of them, a boy no older than nineteen, said my brother's name.

I turned my light off and waded in the dark. It did not help. I could feel them looking.

---

The Smuggler's Tunnel was where the old ship met the new world: timber ribs bracing concrete, a channel of black water chest-deep between them, and above, a lattice of beams that dripped. It climbed. The water flowed against me, which meant the sea was pushing in, which meant there was somewhere above for it to go.

I had gone perhaps fifty meters when the sound changed behind me.

A splash. A pause. Then another splash, heavier, and a long, wet drag — metal on stone, the sound of a hook being pulled along a wall.

I turned off my flashlight. I crouched until the water came to my chin, and I slid into a niche in the wall, and I made myself small, and I did not breathe.

The Shepherd came through the tunnel.

In the dark I could not see it, and that was worse. I could hear it — the wet breathing, the drag of the hook, the soft click of its jaw. It passed within an arm's length of me. The water moved against my chest, pushed by its passing. It stopped.

I closed my eyes. I don't know why. It was not a rational thing. I closed my eyes and held the shard against my chest and felt it humming, warm, a heartbeat in my fist.

The Shepherd stood in the water beside me for a long time. Then it said, in my brother's voice, very softly, as if it were talking to itself:

*"Where did you go, kiddo?"*

And it walked on.

I stayed in the niche until my legs went numb. Then I followed the tunnel up, and the tunnel narrowed, and at the end of it there was a gate — an old iron sluice gate, crank-operated, and beside it a monolith of black basalt carved with the same three-hollow circle I had seen in the village.

The Shepherd came out of the dark behind me.

It did not run. It was in no hurry now; there was nowhere for me to go. It walked up the channel with its coat trailing in the water and its hook scraping the wall, and it filled the tunnel the way a hand fills a glove.

I put my back against the monolith. I held up the shard.

I did not know what I was doing. I want to be clear about that. There was no instruction, no voice, no plan. There was a warm stone in my hand and a thing coming toward me out of the dark, and I did what a person does when they have nothing left: I held up the only light I had.

The shard answered.

It did not glow. It *rang* — a single, enormous note that went through me like a current and out into the tunnel, and the monolith rang back, and the water rang, and the air rang, and everything in that place became one harmonic chord so pure and so loud that the dark itself seemed to split.

The Shepherd stopped. Its jaw opened. It made a sound I had not heard it make before — a high, thin, animal sound, like a dog at a gate — and it stumbled backward into the water and covered its face with its long hands.

I cranked the gate. My arms were shaking so badly I could barely turn the wheel, but the gate rose, and I went through, and I pulled it down behind me, and I kept going.

Behind me, in the tunnel, a chorus of twenty stolen voices spoke at once, and every one of them said the same thing:

*"Don't leave me."*

---

The Abyssal Caverns were a cathedral.

There is no other word. I came out of the tunnel onto a wet basalt ledge, and the ledge was a shelf on the wall of a chasm that went up into darkness and down into darkness, and the chasm was so wide that my light could not find the other side. Water fell somewhere. The air was warm and wet and moving, and the hum was no longer a sound; it was a pressure, a weight on the skin, the feeling of standing too close to a great engine.

And the cavern was full of people.

They sat on ledges, on shelves cut into the stone, on the terraces of a natural amphitheater that spiraled up the walls: hundreds of them, thousands, all seated with their legs crossed and their hands open on their knees, all pale as salt, all turned inward. The Deep Chorus. The old ones of the village, who had walked down into the water at the end of their long lives and let the tide take them.

Their faces were calm. That was the thing I could not get past. In every carven horror I had seen on this island, there had been fear or pain or greed. Here there was none. They looked like people listening to music they loved.

And they were humming.

The chord came from them — all of them, every statue, a thousand small mouths barely moving, holding one warm note that filled the cavern and made the water on the ledge tremble into rings. It was the same chord the shard had rung in the tunnel. It was the same note the island had been singing since I stepped onto the beach.

I stood on the ledge and listened, and for a moment — I am not ashamed to say it — I understood why they had chosen this. The hum was not hostile. It was the sound of being held.

Then I looked down.

The chasm below me was full of black water, and in the black water something moved.

It was vast. It was so vast that my mind refused it at first, translated it into safer things: a reef, a current, a trick of the light. Then it opened.

An eye. A cathedral-sized eye, opening slowly in the black water, its iris a ring of bioluminescent blue that shifted through colors I had no names for, its pupil a darkness deeper than the water around it. It did not look at me the way an animal looks. It looked at me the way the sea looks at a shore — without malice, without mercy, with the patience of something that has been here since before there were eyes to look back.

The hum got louder. The statues' mouths opened wider.

I looked away. It was the hardest thing I did on that island, and it saved my life.

The shard was blazing in my pocket, and in its light I saw the way up: ledges, and ladders cut into the rock, and three bridges of natural stone spanning the chasm at different heights. And at each bridge, a chime — a hanging column of basalt that rang when the air moved.

*Three notes,* Mara's voice said, in my hand, in the shard. *Listen for three notes, and strike the chimes.*

I climbed. The statues watched me go with their calm salt faces. The eye below me did not close.

At the first bridge, I held up the shard and listened. Under the chord, faint as a thought, a three-note phrase: a low note, a rise, a fall. I struck the chime, and it rang the low note, and the bridge steadied under my feet.

At the second bridge, the phrase changed: a rise, a fall, a low. I struck the chime until the air rang clean, and the ledge beyond it stopped wavering.

At the third bridge, the eye below me opened wider, and the pressure in the air became enormous, and I could not hear the phrase at all — only the chord, only the hum, only the sound of a thousand dead villagers singing. I stood on the edge of the bridge with the shard in my hand and my eyes closed and my heart going like a fist on a door, and I waited.

And then I heard it. Three notes, warm and clear, in a woman's voice, close to my ear:

*"Here. Right here. Sing it back."*

I sang it back. My voice was a wreck, cracked and small, but the chime answered it, and the bridge held, and I crossed.

The hatch to Bunker B was set into the cavern wall at the top of the climb, a heavy steel blast door with a wheel and a rusted sign: **SUB-LEVEL B — DET. 9 — AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL.** It was not locked. It had been sealed from the inside, and the seal had been broken from the inside, and when I put my hand on the wheel it turned easily, as if someone had been waiting to open it for a long time.

---

The bunker was small and cold and smelled of diesel and salt and something sweet.

There was a generator junction box, dead. A CCTV camera in the corner, its red light on. A freight elevator door, closed. A bucket. A blanket.

And against the elevator door, sitting with his legs out in front of him and his head back and his hands folded over his chest, was my brother.

He was alive. I knew it before I saw his chest move, because the shard in my pocket turned toward him like a compass needle, and because the whole bunker smelled of him — of the soap he used, of the coffee he drank too much of, of the particular warm dusty smell of the only person in the world who had ever promised to come back.

He was dying. I knew that too. His skin had gone grey and waxy. Black veins crawled up his neck from under his collar, branching like coral. His lips were cracked. His hands, folded over the stone on his chest, were the color of the sea.

The stone was black, and it was beautiful, and it was wrong. It was the size of a man's heart, and its surface moved with a slow blue-white light, and the air around it shimmered the way air shimmers over hot road.

I said his name.

He opened his eyes.

For a moment there was nothing in them. Then something came back — something that had been very far away and had heard a voice it knew — and his face did the thing I had been waiting three days to see. It crumpled. It tried to smile. It failed at both.

"...No," he said. His voice was a wet whisper. "No, no, kiddo. Tell me I'm hallucinating. Tell me you stayed home."

I knelt down. I couldn't speak. I put my hand on his shoulder and he was cold, so cold, and he flinched, and then he leaned into it, and for a moment we were just two people in a room.

"Listen to me," he said. "Don't touch the radio. That voicemail you got? That wasn't me. I never called you."

"I know."

"It's Vane. Julian Vane." He coughed, and the cough was wet and long, and when it was over there was black on his lips. "He built a siphon under the summit to steal immortality from the thing under the island. And he used Mara — Dr. Chen — as the fuse."

The shard in my pocket pulsed, warm.

"When I pulled this third stone out of the circle to save her, the whole circuit blew back into Vane's pod. It burned his mind straight into the island's power grid. He's in the generators. The speakers. The doors." He looked at me, and his eyes were wet and very tired. "But he doesn't have hands."

He lifted the stone. It took everything he had. He pressed it into my hands, and it was warm, and it was heavier than it looked, and it hummed like a struck bell.

"This stone is the only thing keeping me breathing, and it's turning me into salt from the inside out. Take it."

"Elias—"

"Vane needs you to carry this up to the Summit Circle so he can finish his siphon before his body in the upper lab rots into another Shepherd." He gripped my wrist. His fingers were cold and surprisingly strong. "Don't do it. Smash his pod in the Second Facility. And break this stone on the summit rocks. Let me go, kiddo. Promise me."

I looked at him. I looked at the black veins and the wet cough and the stone in my hands, and I understood that this was the last conversation we would ever have, and that he had spent his last strength waiting for someone to have it with.

"I promise," I said.

He closed his eyes. Some of the tension went out of his face. "Good," he said. "Good."

And then he let go.

I don't know how to describe what happened next. There was no light, no sound, no sign. He simply stopped being there — the way a held note stops when the singer runs out of breath. His hand slipped from my wrist. His head turned slightly, as if toward a window I couldn't see. The black veins on his neck went still.

The shard in my pocket blazed, once, a warm white pulse, and in it I heard a woman's voice say, very softly, *"Oh, Elias."*

Then the bunker was quiet.

The camera in the corner whirred and turned toward me.

The PA speaker above the elevator crackled, and for a moment there was my brother's voice, thin and far away: *"Kiddo, please—"*

The speaker clicked. The voice that came out of it next was not my brother's. It was cold and dry and old, and it had a hum underneath it, the way a wire has a hum underneath a recording.

"There. No more splices. It was exhausting speaking through your brother's limited vocabulary anyway."

I stood up. I put the stone in the pack and the pack on my shoulders and I looked at the camera.

"Look at what his nobility bought him," the voice said. "A cold floor in the dark. Do you really think smashing that stone will save him?"

The elevator door opened behind me. Inside, a cage of freight steel, a single light, a button.

"His mind — and Dr. Chen's — are trapped inside the breach above the mountain right now. Break that stone, and the thing in the trench digests them both forever. Put the stone back in my circle, and I can pull them out. The elevator is unlocked. Come up, and see what is at stake."

The speaker clicked off. The elevator waited.

I stepped in. I pressed the button. The doors closed on the bunker, on the blanket, on the place where my brother had been sitting.

The elevator began to rise. It rose for a long time.

# CHAPTER SIX

## The Siphon & the Summit

The elevator opened into a basement that smelled of antiseptic and hot electronics.

It was the Research Center — I knew it from the crates, from the signage, from the logo on the wall: **VANE BIO-MARINE — ADVANCING LIFE.** The basement was concrete and cable trays and emergency lighting that came on as I stepped out, one bank at a time, as if someone were walking through the building ahead of me, turning on the lights.

The corridor ended at a glass wall. Beyond it was the Upper Cable Car Station.

Car 1 hung there, tilted off its track, its windows smashed, its roof torn open. A line of wet barnacle tracks led from the cable housing into the station and then down the corridor toward the labs — a long, glistening trail, like a snail's, but wider.

*That's how it got up here,* I thought. *It climbed the cable.*

The building was warm. The building was awake. My phone buzzed in my pocket and the screen lit up with a phone number that had too many digits, and when I answered it, the voice on the other end was Vane's.

"Don't take the main door. It's sealed. Take the maintenance hatch in the east stair. And don't touch the lights in the corridor — they're mine."

I looked at the corridor. The lights in it were on.

I took the main door. It opened.

The corridor lights went out, all at once, and a hiss began behind me.

Halon. I knew the sound from a dozen safety videos — the white gas that eats fire and doesn't care what else is in the room. It came out of the ceiling vents in jets, freezing white, and the temperature dropped so fast my teeth ached. The far door was sealed with a magnetic lock, and the panel beside it was dead.

I keep trying to remember the order of things and I can't. I remember smashing a vent grille with the butt of the flare gun. I remember crawling through a duct that was full of cold white fog. I remember the taste of it, chemical and sweet, and the sound my own breathing made, loud and ragged in the metal.

I came out into the acoustics lab. Mara's lab.

It was full of her. That was the first thing I thought. Not her body — she had no body anymore — but her *work*: spectrograms pinned to the walls, waveforms on monitors, a whiteboard covered in her handwriting and the same three-hollow circle I had seen carved in stone. On the desk, a rack of cassette tapes, each labeled in her handwriting. And in the middle of the desk, a dictaphone with one tape already cued.

I pressed play.

Her voice came out of it — the same voice that had spoken from the shard, but calmer, more tired, the voice of a woman making a record because she knows the record may be all that is left.

*"Log entry forty-four. Mara Chen. If Elias finds this: don't let Vane near the summit circle."*

A pause. The hiss of the tape.

*"I finished translating the lower cavern glyphs. The entity beneath the island isn't a battery you can tap with a wire. It is a mirror. When the villagers gave their grief willingly, it gave them life. When Captain Locke tried to take that life in chains, the mirror inverted and turned him into the Shepherd."*

Another pause. I could hear her breathing.

*"Vane thinks his Faraday Siphon will filter out the curse and pump the cure into his tank. It won't. Even if he forces a second person to complete the ring, the Deep knows what is stolen. The only way to close the wound is to shatter the third stone—"*

A click, a splice in the tape. Then, quietly:

*"—or step into the ring with open hands and no machine at all."*

The tape ran on into hiss. I stood in her lab with my brother's stone in my pack and the shard warm in my pocket, and I understood that the last words she had left for the world had been cut in two, and that the second half had been meant for me.

Above the desk, a monitor flickered and showed a camera view of a server room. The caption said **A21 — MASTER SERVER.** The lights in the server room were on.

The PA clicked.

"The lab is not a library," Vane said. "You've read enough. The blast gate to the service road is held by three electromagnetic locks, and I control all of them. You cannot open them. Unless you pull the fuses — which will hurt me, so I am telling you not to. Also, the klaxons are very loud. Just so you know."

The PA clicked off.

The server room was two floors up and full of cabinets, each with a red light on its face and a heavy copper bus-fuse in a ceramic housing. Three of them were labeled in Vane's own corporate typeface: **NORTH. EAST. CORE.**

I pulled North. The lights in the north quadrant went out. Vane's voice came out of the speakers, conversational and calm:

"Please don't."

The klaxon went off — a 90-decibel shriek that filled the room and the corridor beyond it and, I was certain, the whole building. I cut my flashlight and crouched behind a cabinet. In the dark, at the far end of the server room, a door opened, and something wet and heavy came in.

I pulled East from inside the dark, by feel, with the klaxon shrieking. The east quadrant went black. The Shepherd's footsteps changed direction.

I crawled. I want to be honest about the crawling. It is not heroic. It is a shameful, animal thing, and I did it with my cheek against the cold floor and my hand over my mouth, and the thing in the dark walked past me close enough that I felt the drag of its coat on the air.

I pulled Core. The whole room went dark, and the klaxon died mid-shriek, and the magnetic locks on the blast gate released with three heavy clunks. The gate at the end of the corridor swung open onto a high, cold wind.

Behind me, in the dark, the Shepherd said, in my brother's voice:

*"Don't go up there, kiddo."*

I went up.

---

The service road climbed the volcanic cone through ash and blasted rock, and above it the sky was wrong.

I could see it even before I reached the summit: a ring. A vast, silent ring of black water hanging in the clouds, perfectly circular, its edges wavering like a heat shimmer, its interior dark with a darkness that had depth. It was not a hole in the sky. It was a *surface* — an inverted ocean, suspended, holding still.

The Black Halo.

Below it, the mountain was lit by it, and everything was silver and grey and very clean.

The Neural Siphon Facility was a concrete dome set into the crater's shoulder. The door was open. Inside, the walls were a nest of superconducting cables, thick as tree trunks, pulsing with a slow blue-white light. They ran from the ceiling down into the center of the room, into a pod — glass and brass and steel, a surgical rotunda, a tank.

Inside the tank was Julian Vane.

He had been a handsome man once; I could see the architecture of it in the bones of his face. Now he was a thing in a jar. His skin was grey and patchy, half of it crusted with black barnacle plates, and his ribs showed through. Copper filaments had grown out of the ports in his spine and woven themselves into the cables above him. His jaw hung slightly wrong, as if the hinge had come loose. One eye was open and blue. The other was black — all black, no white, a depth of black that made my stomach lurch.

Six speakers around the room spoke at once. His voice came out of all of them, and out of the water in the tank, and out of the cables themselves.

"Don't look at the glass like that. Look at the monitors. Six hours left before the black cartilage reaches my brainstem."

I walked toward the pod. I don't know why. Perhaps because a thing in a tank is a thing in a tank, and I had been afraid of so many things that night, and this was the first one I could see all of.

"Do you know what it feels like to be stretched across four miles of copper wire while your body turns into barnacles and brine in a jar?"

I stopped at the console beside the pod. It was a physical thing — a rotary switch, a lever, a row of gauges. The label read **SIPHON ROUTING — CONDUIT TO POD — ACTIVE.**

"Captain Locke was a primitive brute with iron chains. I built a clean filter! It was working — until your brother tore the third stone out and left me half-digested by this thing!"

The shard in my pocket was humming. The stone in my pack was humming. The cables were humming. The whole room was a chord.

I put my hand on the rotary switch.

"Don't touch that console!" The speakers cracked with volume. "Listen to me! If you complete the circuit with the siphon running, it takes sixty seconds to flush my nervous system and release the breach. Sixty seconds, and you can walk down to the beach. Smash that stone, and you murder me — and you erase whatever is left of Elias in the dark!"

I looked at him. He looked back with one blue eye and one black one, and something in the black one moved, the way a fish moves under ice.

"You're lying," I said.

"No," he said. "I'm *bargaining.* It's different."

The console had three positions and a lever. I could leave the siphon routed to the pod. I could arm the pod for overload and let the breach do the rest. I could pull the lever and cut his cables out of the circle entirely.

My hand rested on the switch. The cables pulsed under the floor like veins. Somewhere above me the Black Halo was turning, and somewhere below me the sea was rising around the Black Stone, and in the dark of the bunker my brother's body was already going cold.

Whatever I decided, I would have to decide it in the circle, with the third stone in my hands and the sky open above me.

I took my hand off the console.

"Wait," Vane said. His voice was thin now. "Wait. Don't go up there without deciding. Do you hear me? Don't—"

I climbed the crater steps.

---

The Summit Crater Circle was open to the sky.

The crater was a shallow bowl of black basalt, and the ring stood in the center of it: twelve standing stones, and between them, on a raised altar, three sockets. Two of them held stones — black, blazing, their veins so bright they threw shadows across the crater floor. The third held nothing.

Above, the Black Halo hung in the clouds, and the clouds moved around it the way water moves around a stone.

Offshore, four hundred meters below, the Black Stone blazed like a lighthouse at the bottom of the world.

I took Tide-Stone #3 out of my pack.

It was heavier than it had any right to be. It was warm. It hummed against my palms, and the hum matched the hum of the two stones in the circle, and the three notes braided together into a chord that made my eyes water and my teeth ache and my heart slow down to the rhythm of something much larger than a heart.

I stepped into the circle.

At the rim of the crater, a silhouette climbed over the standing stones.

It came up out of the dark below the rim the way a memory comes back — slowly, then all at once — and it was enormous against the silver light of the Halo, its coat streaming, its long hands open. It climbed into the circle, and it stood, and it spoke, and its voice was not one voice. It was twenty. It was a dead corporal and a dead sailor and a dead survey team and a dead woman in a burned village, all of them speaking in unison:

*"GIVE IT BACK."*

I ran for the first totem.

The Shepherd came after me.

I want to say the final confrontation was a fight. It was not. It was a chase around a ring of standing stones under an ocean that hung in the sky, with a thing that had been hunting this island for two hundred and forty years, and I was a small warm animal with a shard of salt in my hand.

The first totem was at the north of the circle. I put the shard against the carving, and the shard rang, and the flash went out like a wave of white light, and the Shepherd staggered back with its hands over its face, and its jaws opened, and it screamed in twenty voices.

I ran for the second. It caught me across the circle — not with a hand, but with its *presence*, a wave of cold and pressure that made my legs give out, and I went down on the basalt with the stone clutched to my chest. It stood over me. Its jaw unhinged. Its coat dripped into my face.

*"Kiddo,"* it said, gently, in my brother's voice. *"Kiddo, I'm here. Just give it to me."*

I hit it with the shard.

The flash was close and hard and terrible; the Shepherd reeled back, and I rolled out from under it, and I got to the second totem, and I rang it, and the light drove it to the rim.

The third totem was at the south, nearest the altar. I ran for it with the Shepherd coming behind me. I could hear its feet on the stone, the long dragging stride, the wet breathing. I could hear it saying my name — not in my brother's voice now, but in my *own* voice, the voice I use in my own head, the voice I had never heard out loud.

I put the shard to the third totem and rang it.

The flash went out across the crater, and the Shepherd went down on one knee at the rim, and for a moment — a single, clear moment — the coat seemed to hang correctly on its body. It looked up at the clearing sky above the Halo. It looked at me. Its jaw moved, and it said something I could not hear, and then it lowered its head.

I stood at the altar with Tide-Stone #3 in my hands.

The sky was opening. The Black Halo was beginning to turn, slowly, like a great wheel, and the hum was rising, and everything on the island was waiting.

There were three things I could do.

# THE ENDINGS

## Ending One — The Severed Cord

I had set the console to overload before I climbed the steps — reversed the polarity, left the pod armed to burn.

I raised the stone above my head and brought it down on the basalt altar.

It did not shatter at once. It resisted, the way a thought resists being spoken, and then it split with a sound that was not a crack but a *note* — a pure, agonizing harmonic that went through my arms and my chest and my teeth and out across the crater. The two halves fell apart, and a shockwave of white light came out of them and rolled over me, and the world went silent.

Above the crater, the Black Halo collapsed inward like a punctured lung. It did not tear. It *folded*, the way water folds when a stone is pulled out of it, and the clouds rushed in, and the night was suddenly just a night again.

Down in the facility, every transformer exploded. I heard it through the stone — a long cascade of blue arcs and shrieking metal — and then Vane's voice came out of the crater speakers, high and broken and human, screaming one long vowel that dissolved into static and then into nothing.

At the rim, the Shepherd stopped mid-stride.

It did not fall. It stood very still, its coat hanging slack, and then it looked up at the clearing sky, and its jaw moved once, and it dropped to its knees. Its head bowed. Its shoulders came down. And it stayed there, bowed, as the first grey light of dawn touched the crater rim — a thing that had been walking for two hundred and forty years and had finally been given leave to stop.

Far out past the point, the Black Stone was sinking.

I watched it go. The sea closed over it without a ripple, the way water closes over a hand, and the hum that had been in my teeth since the beach faded to a whisper and then to nothing, and the island was quiet for the first time.

I sat down on the crater steps facing south. My hands were shaking. The pale shard in my pocket was cool now, and dark.

Dawn came up grey and ordinary. The clouds were just clouds. Gulls — actual gulls, small and stupid and alive — turned over the water.

Far out, a tiny yellow lamp came around the point.

The Captain's boat.

I took out my cracked phone and looked at my reflection in the dark glass. My face was grey with ash and salt, and my eyes were my own, and there was a faint dark ring around each pupil that had not been there before.

I put the phone away and went down to the beach.

The boat came in. The Captain did not say a word about the Black Stone, or the crater, or the state of me. He looked at my face for a long moment, and then he looked away, and he said, "You want the stern. Wind's better."

I sat in the stern and watched the island shrink. The Black Stone was gone. The mountain was just a mountain. Somewhere up there was a bowed figure in a rotting coat that would never move again, and somewhere in the dark under the sea was a woman's voice that was no longer calling.

I had kept my promise.

The island did not let me go entirely. I still hear the hum sometimes, at night, in the space between one wave and the next. But I am alive, and my brother's stone is broken, and the thing in the trench is dreaming again without a machine to feed on.

That is as much of a happy ending as this island allows.

---

## Ending Two — The New Shepherd

I looked at the two blazing stones in the circle, and the empty socket between them, and I thought about a voice in a jar saying *sixty seconds*, and a woman's voice on a tape saying *it is a mirror*, and I made the wrong choice for the right reasons.

I had left the siphon routed to his pod. I had not touched the lever.

I slid Tide-Stone #3 into the socket.

All three stones blazed violet-black. A pillar of resonance shot up through the ring and down through the mountain along the superconducting cables, and the Black Halo above the crater began to descend, and the hum became a roar.

Over the speakers, Julian Vane gasped.

"It's working! My hands — I can feel my hands inside the pod! The decay is reversing!"

His voice was young. That was the thing that made my skin crawl. It was young and joyful and human.

Then the tone inverted.

It is difficult to describe. Every sound in the world turned inside out — the roar became a shriek, the chord became a scream, the light became a color I had never seen and could not look at. The Cosmic Law took effect the way a hand closes: *taken by force, it corrupts.*

Down in the facility, the glass pod shattered.

Over the PA, Vane's laughter turned into a wet, choking sound, and then into a long, guttural scrape, and then into nothing. The crater horns carried it up to us — the sound of a man's jaw unhinging, of cartilage erupting through a spine, of something being remade.

Beside me at the rim, the old Shepherd exhaled.

It was a long, slow breath of sea-mist, and it went on for a long time, longer than any human lungs could hold. And then Captain Alistair Locke crumbled — coat, buttons, bones, and all — into a pile of empty naval cloth and coarse white salt, and the wind took the salt away.

His replacement had been found.

The Black Halo descended over the circle. The dark water came down slowly, without hurry, the way a hand comes down to close an eye, and I felt it on my skin, cold and vast and patient.

The last thing I saw was a tall, twisted silhouette rising from the shattered dome below, its jaw hanging open, its coat — Vane's white lab coat, fused now to something else — streaming in the wind.

Then the water closed over my vision.

And my voice, which had been mine for my whole life, joined the stolen chorus in his throat.

Somewhere very far away, a woman's voice said: *I told you. Only what is given.*

---

## Ending Three — Willing Communion

I can't tell you exactly when I decided. I can tell you that it was not in the crater. It was earlier — in a burned street with a dead woman's shard in my hand, or on a ledge above a sleeping eye, or in a bunker with my brother's cold hand in mine.

I set the stone in the socket. I had severed Vane's cables in the facility below. The ring was just a ring now, and the sky was just a sky with an ocean in it.

Then I walked into the center of the circle, and I held up the pale shard in both hands, and I opened my hands.

Vane's voice came out of the dying speakers, thin and furious and small:

"What are you doing?! You disconnected the pod! Without the filter, it will take your mind!"

I did not answer him. I was not there for him.

The hum rose. It rose through my feet and my legs and my chest, and it was not a pressure anymore, and it was not a threat. It was a question, asked in the only language the island had ever spoken. It asked what I was willing to give.

I thought about my brother. I thought about the letter in my pocket and the voicemail on my phone and the way he had folded his hands over the stone and waited in the dark for someone to come. I thought about the villagers who had walked into the water with their faces calm, and the healing wall, and the wound the island had been carrying since 1784.

I offered all of it. My grief. My memories. My name. The small, bright, stubborn thing I had always called *me*.

Dr. Mara Chen's voice came out of the shard — warm, clear, close:

*"Only what is given willingly."*

The storm stopped.

It stopped in an instant, the way a held breath stops when it is released. The Black Halo above the crater did not tear. It rippled — once, gently, like a calm tide pool when someone trails a hand through it — and then it washed down over me, and it was warm, and it was golden-white, and it was the most beautiful thing I have ever seen.

---

I woke on the beach at dawn.

The sun was coming up over the pines, and the sand was warm, and the sea was quiet. The tide was out. The Black Stone was gone.

Beside me, on the dry sand at the edge of the trail, my brother was lying on his back with his eyes closed and his hands folded on his chest, breathing slowly and steadily.

The black veins were gone from his neck. His skin was the color of a man who had been sick and was getting better. His chest rose and fell. He was alive.

I sat up. I tried to say his name.

"Elias."

What came out of my throat was a hum.

It was low and warm and in tune with the sea, and it had no words in it, and it was the same note the island had been singing since I stepped onto the sand. I put my hand to my throat and felt it there — a resonance, a chord, a small permanent tide.

My hands were pale. They were translucent as sea-glass, and when the sunlight came through them it made a pattern on the sand.

The Captain's boat came around the point, its yellow lamp off now in the daylight, and I watched it nose toward the shore. The Captain would find Elias breathing. He would take him home. He would hear the laugh of the daughter he had been so careful to see again.

I stood at the water's edge and watched my brother wake up. He opened his eyes. He looked at the sky. He turned his head and saw me standing in the surf, and his face did the thing I had been waiting three days to see — it crumpled, and it tried to smile, and this time it managed.

"Hey," he said. "Hey. Kiddo."

I could not answer him.

I did not need to. I raised one pale hand, and he raised his, and between us the morning tide came in, warm and quiet, humming the same three notes.

Then I turned and walked into the surf.

The water was warm. It was warm all the way down. Somewhere below me, in the dark, a thousand salt statues sat in a circle with their faces calm and their hands open, holding one long chord, and the chord had room in it.

I did not look back. I did not need to. The last thing I heard from the shore was my brother's voice, shouting my name, and the last thing I felt was the hum, rising to meet me, warm as a held hand.

I went down to join the Deep Chorus.

# APPENDIX

## The Cosmic Law

**The Deep only harmonizes with what opens itself willingly. Taken by force, its gift inverts into an eternal hunger.**

The entire island is a record of this law.

- **Willing Communion.** The villagers of the old island opened the ring once a year, offered their grief and their ego into the hum, and were healed. Their sick rose whole. Their old walked into the flooded caverns and became salt statues, still singing, still together.
- **Parasitism.** Captain Alistair Locke tried to bind the same power in an iron cage and harvest it without surrender. The mirror inverted. He became the Shepherd, and the island kept him.
- **The Siphon.** Julian Vane built a machine to do what Locke did, but with better engineering and a human fuse. It did not work. It could not work. The law is not a rule. It is a law.

## A Note on the Three Stones

The **Three Tide-Stones** are meteoric black glass — tuning forks for a dimensional wound. Placed in the sockets of the ancient ring while the Black Stone rises, they open a Resonance Breach to the Sleeper.

The **Pale Shards** are calcified salt-crystals left behind by the tide after a willing communion. They heal. They hum at Mara's frequency. At a resonance totem, a shard can be made to ring — a harmonic flash bright enough to drive the Shepherd back.

The **Black Stone** is not a stone. It is the calcified crown of the Sleeper's spire, and when it stands dry, the veil between the island and the trench is thin enough for a voice to cross it.

If it calls you by name, don't answer.

Not even if it sounds like your own blood.
